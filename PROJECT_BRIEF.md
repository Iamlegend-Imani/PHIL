# PHIL: Personal Health Intelligence Layer

## Project brief

**Builder:** Imani Kirika  
**Status:** Functional concept prototype  
**Live prototype:** <https://phil-health-map.imani-kirika116.chatgpt.site>  
**Domain:** Patient context, visit preparation, human-centered health technology

## One-line concept

PHIL is a patient-controlled context layer that converts fragmented health information and lived experience into a clear, source-aware brief for a better clinical conversation.

## The opportunity

Health information is commonly organized around institutions, data types, and individual encounters. The person must then carry the connective tissue between them: what changed, what they tried, what they can afford, what their life permits, and what matters most now.

The working need hypothesis is that this reconstruction burden can make already-limited visit time less useful. PHIL explores whether a patient-controlled preparation layer could reduce that burden without attempting to automate diagnosis or treatment.

This is an unvalidated hypothesis. The current prototype demonstrates a proposed workflow and the questions I would test, not proof that the need or solution has been clinically validated.

## The proposed workflow

1. **Gather the fragments.** The patient brings together their story, current medication list, mock record extracts, daily-life context, and practical constraints.
2. **Make patterns visible.** Events are placed in sequence with source labels and uncertainty intact.
3. **Prepare the conversation.** The patient selects the priorities and questions that belong in a concise visit brief.
4. **Keep judgment human.** A person verifies identity, source accuracy, patient intent, uncertainty, and the absence of unsupported clinical claims before the brief is approved for sharing.

## What I built

- A responsive six-view interactive prototype
- A whole-person Context Map
- A source ledger with verification states
- A Health Timeline that separates sequence from causation
- A patient-controlled question builder
- A printable Visit Preparation Brief
- A pre-share verification gate
- A visible human decision record
- A Method & Safety section embedded in the interface
- A fictional scenario, methodology, safety protocol, case study, roadmap, and practitioner review guide

## Design principles

**Nothing exists in isolation.** Biology, behavior, environment, access, and lived experience interact.

**The patient is not a data container.** Their language, goals, affordability, and capacity are part of the context.

**Source matters.** Every detail should retain its origin, date, and verification state.

**A pattern is not a conclusion.** Timing and variation can support better questions without establishing diagnosis or causation.

**Capability can be automated. Accountability cannot.** Final interpretation and care decisions remain human-owned.

## Intended user and moment

The concept is designed for an adult preparing for a non-emergency primary or specialty care visit involving a complex or evolving story. It is not designed for urgent triage, autonomous monitoring, or direct-to-consumer treatment decisions.

## Safety boundary

PHIL does not diagnose, predict disease, prescribe, recommend medication or supplements, interpret lab results, prioritize emergencies, or replace a qualified professional. All prototype content is fictional. No personal health information is collected or stored.

## Evidence status

The interface and documentation are built and publicly inspectable. The patient need, workflow fit, usability, practitioner value, and safety performance remain to be tested. External practitioner review is pending and will not be represented as complete until attributable feedback is received.

## Next validation questions

- At what point before a visit does context reconstruction become most burdensome?
- Which details are genuinely useful to a practitioner, and which create noise?
- Can a patient review and correct the generated brief without excessive effort?
- What provenance and uncertainty cues are necessary for responsible use?
- Does the workflow fit existing clinical practice without creating a second record?
- Which populations could be excluded by language, access, disability, or health-literacy demands?
